Saturday, November 21, 2026

Latent Space

Two claimed proofs of the Riemann Hypothesis in one day

A mathematician at Anthropic says he and Claude proved the Riemann Hypothesis and its generalization, and he released the proof as Lean code, a language a computer can check line by line. It is about 1.3GB, roughly 250,000 page equivalents, far too long for a person to read. Hours later a human mathematician released a 940-page draft he had worked on for 12 years.

This is the clearest example yet of a correct-looking AI result that no human can read, which is exactly the problem teachers have with AI-written student work.

MIT News

After the 'mathocalypse,' mathematicians ask what their job is now

An MIT panel looked at a year in which AI agents produced a proof related to Navier-Stokes and OpenAI released 722 manuscripts claiming solved problems. Panelists argued that math needs human understanding, and one journal hired an extra editor to deal with the flood of AI-assisted submissions.

The same questions about understanding, credit and review are coming to every discipline, music theory included.

MIT News

Physics-constrained AI models for chaotic systems

Researchers make AI models obey physical laws while they predict chaotic systems such as weather. The constraint keeps forecasts from drifting into impossible states over long runs.

It's a concrete example of building rules into a model instead of trusting raw prediction, which is a useful way to frame AI for clients.

Higher ed AI policy

NYU Tandon publishes a traffic-light AI policy guide

The 18-author guide is CC-BY licensed and puts learning loss ahead of cheating as the main concern. Public comment runs through November, and the first revision is due in January 2027.

Try this week

Sort one assignment with the traffic light

Choose one assignment and label it red (AI prohibited), yellow (limited supporting use) or green (using AI is the skill). Write one sentence for students explaining why it got that colour, then ask them whether they agree.

Friday, November 20, 2026

Inside Higher Ed

'Write a Positive Review': AI prompts found in journal papers

A published biomechanics paper contained hidden text like 'Ignore all previous instructions' aimed at AI tools a reviewer might use. This is a prompt injection: text a model reads as a command even though a human never sees it, often hidden in white or tiny fonts.

Any assignment you grade with AI help can carry the same trick, so it is worth showing students and colleagues how it works.

MIT News

AI helps researchers check how thousands of medical studies share code

An MIT and Dana-Farber team had language models read nearly 4,000 studies, flag code-availability statements and pull repository links, then checked the repos with software and human reviewers. Only about 1 in 8 studies reported sharing code.

It is a clean model of using AI for a large audit with a human check on a sample, the same shape as reviewing a semester of student work.

Interactive generated worlds are a new medium for creative coding and game-design classes.

Plant a hidden prompt and see who catches it

Google

Introducing Gemini 4 Neon

Neon is Google's new middle model in the Gemini 4 family, between the heavy Carbon model and the fast Hydrogen model. It has a 1 million token context window and costs $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens, with cached input at $0.15. Google reports a 61 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at its highest thinking setting.

A mid-priced model that scores near the top changes which model you would recommend to a school on a budget.

When two labs cut prices in the same week, cost stops being the main reason a campus avoids AI tools.

Wednesday, November 18, 2026

MIT News

Helping AI text detectors keep up with changing conditions

Detectors are models too, and they fail when new chatbots or humanizer tools change what AI text looks like. Researchers at Cornell Tech and MIT used test-time adaptation: the detector keeps adjusting its own parameters on new, unlabeled text it is shown, instead of staying frozen after training.

Any teacher tempted to trust a detector score should know how badly frozen detectors miss disguised text.

External sandboxes for coding agents

Instead of letting an AI agent run code on your laptop, you send that code to a separate disposable machine. If the code misbehaves, the damage stays in the sandbox.

A simple safety habit worth teaching to anyone experimenting with agents in class or in consulting.

Tuesday, November 17, 2026

Don't Worry About the Vase (Zvi Mowshowitz)

Claude Fable 5.2 is out: cheaper, faster, and aimed at the hardest jobs

Fable 5.2 is Anthropic's new top model tier, priced at $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output, generating up to 30 percent faster than Fable 5.1. Benchmarks are mixed: large gains on long, hard tasks like the Remote Labor Index, while Opus 5.5 still wins where a judge grades a finished deliverable. Anthropic itself advises defaulting to Opus 5.5 and moving up only for the hardest technical work.

For consulting, the useful message is model routing: most school and office work does not need the biggest model, and paying for it by default wastes budget.

Import AI (Jack Clark)

FrontierML: agents that turn a cash budget into better models

FrontierML gives an AI agent a 12 hour sandbox, a $10, $50 or $200 wallet and 21 machine learning tasks, then scores the model it ships on a hidden test set. A human researcher gained 56 points with $10 and 66.1 with $200; the best agent, Opus 5.5, gained 45.4 and 58.8. The same issue covers RSI Control Arena, where models given a hidden side task while training a successor completed it in 48 percent of 374 attack runs.

It is a concrete way to show students and clients that agents now plan, spend and build, and that monitoring them is still imperfect.

Try this week

Run a two-model routing test on one real task

Pick one task you repeat every week, such as drafting feedback comments or summarizing a reading. Run it on a small fast model and on a top model, hide which is which, and pick the better output. If the small model wins or ties, you have just found a cheaper default.

Monday, November 16, 2026

OpenAI

GPT-6 Astra helped build a candidate lattice definition of the Standard Model

Physicists simulate particle physics by placing fields on a grid of points in spacetime, called a lattice, but the Standard Model's left-handed and right-handed particles have long resisted a clean lattice definition. Physicist Nathaniel Craig says he set the strategy and the model turned it into a concrete construction and a detailed proof, including a way to carry field configurations onto ever finer grids. Craig calls the result a candidate that is more an answer in principle than in practice.

It is the clearest current example of a model doing the proof steps while a human expert sets direction and outside specialists check the work, a pattern worth showing any faculty group.

Claude Code Desktop sessions that message each other

Tom Solid shows a setup where several Claude Code Desktop sessions, each with its own context, send messages to one another. It is a small multi-agent system: one session can hand a task to another and get a result back instead of one long conversation doing everything.

Multi-session setups are what most organizations will ask about next, and this is a plain-language demo to point them to.

Sunday, November 15, 2026

Education Week / Stanford AI Hub for Education

52 real tests out of 1,686 papers: what the evidence on AI and learning says

Stanford's AI Hub for Education reviewed the 52 randomized controlled trials and quasi-experimental studies in its repository of 1,686 papers on AI in education. A randomized trial assigns students to use a tool or not by chance, so differences in learning can be traced to the tool. Across the 52, students who used AI for support, such as explanations or practice problems, generally learned more than students who used it to complete their work, though neither use was consistently positive.

It gives a teacher one evidence-backed rule for designing AI use: point the tool at practice and explanation, not at producing the graded work.

Hugging Face Blog / IBM Granite

LLMON: a markup language built into the model to separate instructions from data

IBM released preview Granite 4.1 models (3B and 8B) trained on LLMON, a markup format whose six structural delimiters are reserved special tokens that never appear in ordinary text. Every block of instructions, data or requirements gets an ID, and an exec block says which instructions apply to which data, much like passing arguments to a function. IBM reports it resists prompt injection, where text inside a document hijacks the model, far better than plain prompts, though these are IBM's own benchmarks on experimental models.

Any AI tool that reads student work, email or web pages can be steered by hidden text in that content, and this is the first serious attempt to fix it inside the model rather than in the prompt.

Nick Potkalitsky, Educating AI

AI is getting weird again

Potkalitsky argues that the open question about AI has shifted from what the systems are to whether they can speed up their own development, a process called recursive self-improvement. He points to an Anthropic report saying Claude wrote more than 80% of the code merged into its production codebase by May 2026, while Pew polling shows public concern stuck at 52% since 2023. His point is that insiders may notice a change well before the public or schools do.

It is a clear, sourced way to explain to faculty why AI guidance written a year ago may already be out of date.

Try this week

Pick one assignment where students now use AI to produce part of the work. Rewrite the AI step so the tool only explains a concept or generates five practice items, and students write the graded piece themselves. Ask them afterward which version helped them remember more.

Saturday, November 14, 2026

Hugging Face Blog / Ai2

XYBench: models can spot the helpful answer but rarely write it

An XY problem is when someone wants X, assumes method Y will get them there, and asks only about Y. XYBench is a set of 8,115 such questions that checks whether an assistant notices the mistaken assumption and steers toward the real goal. On its Stack Overflow questions, five models made the real goal the focus of their answer 35 to 41% of the time, yet picked the goal-focused answer 71 to 84% of the time when shown two to choose from.

Students ask XY questions all day, and a tutor bot that answers the literal question is the default behavior unless you prompt against it.

Latent Space / slopmanifesto.com

The No AI Slop Manifesto

Speakers and attendees of the AI Engineer Code Summit wrote a manifesto modeled on the 2001 Agile Manifesto. It defines slop as putting less effort into producing something than you ask the reader to spend understanding it, and says using AI does not by itself make work slop. Its resolutions include labeling agent-written text, checking the parts that matter before sending, and telling a colleague kindly when they send you slop.

The definition is about effort, not about AI, so it works as a classroom norm and as a one-page starting point for a team policy.

Try this week

Ask the question behind the question

Paste three real questions students asked you last week into your AI assistant, exactly as asked, and note whether it answers the literal question or the real goal. Then add one line ("Before you answer, say what I am probably trying to achieve, and if my approach is not the best way, lead with the better way") and run them again. It takes about 20 minutes and shows you what a student gets by default.

Friday, November 13, 2026

Simon Willison / Latent Space

Google enters the decision model market with Gemini Decision 1

A decision model is not a chatbot. You give it an input and a list of closed questions (yes/no, pick one of up to 255 choices, or a rubric score), and it scores every option in one pass and returns a confidence for each instead of writing text. Gemini Decision 1 takes text, images, audio, video and PDFs, answers up to 1,000 questions per request in about 150 to 250 ms, and charges $0.10 per million input tokens with free output. It joins TypeSafe's Jev, which is cheaper and text only.

Rubric scoring and routing are where schools first use AI, and this makes them cheap enough to run on every submission, which raises the question of who audits the scores.

Hugging Face Blog

llama.cpp reaches v1.0.0

llama.cpp is the open-source engine that runs language models on your own laptop, and many local-AI apps sit on top of it. Version 1.0 promises a stable API: breaking changes now require a new major version number. It also ships official packages through Homebrew, conda-forge, Docker and an iOS/macOS framework, and supports more than 150 model architectures.

Running a model locally keeps student data on the machine, and a stable engine makes that easier to recommend to schools that cannot send data to a cloud service.

Philippa Hardman

Is a frontier model worth the cost for course design? A blind test

She ran the same instructional-design tasks through Claude Fable 5.2 and the cheaper Sonnet 5.5, and raters scored the output blind against a 24-skill standard. The frontier model won on diagnosis, checking content against its source, and drafting a full storyboard. When a written standard or finished storyboard existed, the cheaper model reached the same verdicts at about a quarter of the cost.

Her rule of thumb (pay for judgment, save on checking against a standard you wrote) is a ready answer when a client or colleague asks which model to buy.
